# The norm-one subgroup: where the domination argument fails

A correction to round 2, and a working method in the gap.

## The gap in the argument

| group | order | is it divisible by `p-1`? |
|---|---|---|
| full unit group of `F_{p^d}` | `p^d - 1` | yes -- hence dominated |
| **norm-one subgroup** | `(p^d - 1)/(p - 1)` | **no** |

For `d = 2` the norm-one order is `p + 1`. So the question is whether primes with `p-1` rough and `p+1` smooth actually exist in useful density.

Sampling smooth `p+1` candidates, **6** such primes turned up in 33 attempts. They are not exotic.

**6 of 6** are factored by the norm-one method and by nothing on the `p-1` side. The round-2 claim of strict domination is refuted.

Base retries are not optional: for `d = 2` the root only lands in `F_{p^2}` when `base^2 - 4` is a non-residue mod `p`, otherwise it falls back into `F_p` and the method silently degenerates to `p-1`. That is a coin flip per base, so a single failing base means nothing. (This experiment's first draft tried seven bases that all happened to be residues and wrongly recorded a failure.)

### The statistic is still a binomial sum

Worth noting where this leaves the Pascal framing. The Lucas sequence driving the method has a closed form

```
V_k(a,1) = sum_j (-1)^j * (k/(k-j)) * C(k-j, j) * a^(k-2j)
```

Verified on **540** cases, **0** mismatches. So the statistic that finally breaks the symmetry between `p` and `q` *is* a binomial sum -- just not a sum along row `n`. What changes is not leaving binomials behind; it is that the roots are constrained to multiply to `1`, which is what pins the order to `p+1` instead of `p-1`.

### Degree `d > 2`

| degree d | norm-one order | split rate, random semiprimes, bound 150 |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | (p^2 - 1)/(p - 1) | 12/12 |
| 3 | (p^3 - 1)/(p - 1) | 8/12 |
| 4 | (p^4 - 1)/(p - 1) | 9/12 |

Higher `d` covers `Phi_e(p)` for every `e | d` at once, because the defining polynomial usually factors mod `p` and the norm vanishes if *any* component's order divides `M`. More coverage per run, at higher cost per run.

### What this does and does not change

It **does** answer the question round 2 left open -- "find a statistic of the first digit that is asymmetric in the prime factors" -- in the affirmative. The norm-one construction is exactly such a statistic, and it is reachable without knowing `p`.

It does **not** give polynomial time, and the reason is worth stating precisely, because it is the next real obstruction:

| method | group | order | varies with a parameter we control? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pollard `p-1` | `F_p*` | `p - 1` | no |
| Williams / norm-one, degree `d` | `ker N` in `F_{p^d}*` | `Phi_d(p)`-ish | no -- fixed once `p` is |
| ECM | `E(F_p)` | `p + 1 - t` | **yes** -- a new order per curve |

Every method here has a **rigid** group order: once `p` is fixed, so is the number whose smoothness decides success. You get one lottery ticket per `p`, and if `Phi_d(p)` is rough for all small `d` you are stuck. ECM's advantage is not a better group -- it is a *family* of groups, one per curve, so you can keep drawing fresh orders for the same `p`. That is what takes it from `Theta(smoothness luck)` to `L[1/2]`.

So the open problem sharpens again: **is there a family of AKS-ring-like objects whose group order varies with a parameter, at fixed `p`?** The norm-one construction gives one order per `(p, d)`; elliptic curves give unboundedly many per `p`. Nothing found here bridges that.
