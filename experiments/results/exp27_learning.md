The learner is not blind. It recovers the planted parity (100%), the residue `N` genuinely determines (100%: `N = 1 (mod 4)` exactly when `p = q (mod 4)`, the `ell = 4` case of round 13), and a real but partial signal in the top bits of `p` (0.820 against 0.746), which is the size of `N` seen through `p ~ sqrt(N/r)`.

Below the top bits there is nothing. For bits 10 down to 1 of `p` and its residues mod 3 and 4, the best test-set gain over the majority guess is +0.0000, and the strongest single correlation is 1.00 times the maximum expected from noise alone over 1,000 characters.

The strongest correlation is 1.07 times the typical noise maximum -- maxima over 92,170 characters land on either side of it -- and the best gain over the majority guess is +0.0063, within two standard errors (+-0.0082) of the test accuracy. Characters that cross the selection threshold by chance do not generalise: where they are used, test accuracy does not rise above the majority guess.

What this rules out is narrow and worth stating exactly: structure of degree at most four in the bits of `N` that predicts the low or middle bits of `p`. A learner with more capacity could in principle find higher-degree structure that this one would miss; the controls establish only that the pipeline -- sampling, splitting, scoring -- registers signal when there is some. The picture matches every other round: `N` carries its size and its symmetric residues, and nothing that tells `p` from `q`.
