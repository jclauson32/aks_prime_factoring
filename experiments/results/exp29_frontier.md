The sieve is faster than rho from 80 bits on and factors the 110-bit numbers in a median 6.3 s of pure Python. Rho grows by 6.0x per ten bits (`N^(1/4)` predicts 5.7); the sieve by 2.7x over the same kind of range. That is the sub-exponential `L[1/2]` at work: in theory its growth factor per ten bits keeps shrinking, and at these sizes it is already the smaller one.

What the sieve has that the central column (round 15) lacks is the whole difference. Both manufacture a zero divisor from the *sign* of a square root. The central column asks for one Legendre symbol and pays `sqrt p` for it. The sieve never evaluates a Legendre symbol mod `p` at all: it collects `x^2 = Q(x) (mod N)` with `Q(x)` small enough -- about `sqrt N` times the sieve length -- to be smooth unusually often, and lets linear algebra over `F_2` find a product that is a square on both sides. Its cost is the density of smooth numbers, the same quantity that caps the order methods at `L[1/2]` (Proposition 17), and the number field sieve pushes it to `L[1/3]` by making the numbers smaller still.

Every mechanism a polynomial-time algorithm could use is on the table of round 14. Smoothness-based ones are sub-exponential and not polynomial, because smooth numbers of size `N^c` are too rare for any fixed `c > 0`. The others, measured here and in twenty rounds before, sit at `N^(1/4)` or worse. A polynomial-time method along smoothness lines would need relations carried by numbers of size `N^(o(1))`, and no known construction produces them.
