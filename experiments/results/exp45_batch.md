# Amortisation: what a batch costs that one number does not

Round 39. Batch smoothness detection by product trees, against trial division one number at a time.

## The control: same answers

Both routines are asked which of a batch of numbers are 5000-smooth. The batch version multiplies every prime together, reduces that product down a tree of the numbers, squares each remainder enough times to kill every prime power, and takes a gcd. It has to agree with trial division on every number, and it also has to return the right smooth *part*, which is checked by dividing it out.

The control runs at 32 bits rather than the 64 used for timing below, because a 64-bit number is 5000-smooth about once in five thousand: a test where both routines answer "no" to everything checks almost nothing. Two dozen numbers built out of small primes are planted in each batch as well, so the positive answers are exercised too.

| batch size | smooth found | agrees with trial division | smooth part correct |
|---|---|---|---|
| 88 | 29 | 88/88 | 88/88 |
| 280 | 54 | 280/280 | 280/280 |
| 1048 | 124 | 1048/1048 | 1048/1048 |


## The cost, per number

Both methods are timed on the same batches of 64-bit numbers. Trial division's cost per number should be flat -- it does not know the batch exists. The tree's should fall, because the expensive object, the product of all the primes, is built once and then walked down.

| batch size | product tree (us per number) | trial division (us per number) | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32 | 7.1 | 32.7 | 4.6x |
| 128 | 4.2 | 31.9 | 7.6x |
| 512 | 3.8 | 32.6 | 8.6x |
| 2048 | 4.8 | 31.6 | 6.6x |
| 8192 | 6.9 | 31.2 | 4.5x |

The per-number cost falls and then rises again, and the best batch here is 512 numbers at 3.8 us each, 8.6 times faster than trial division -- against 4.6x at 32 and 4.5x at 8192. The fall is the amortisation: the product of all the primes is a fixed cost spread over more numbers. The rise is arithmetic on the tree's own nodes, whose root is the product of the whole batch -- 524 thousand bits at the last row -- and multiplying numbers that size is superlinear. Real implementations do exactly what this table says to do: run the algorithm on blocks of the best size rather than on everything at once.

Trial division's per-number cost varies by a factor of 1.05 across a 256-fold range of batch sizes, which is measurement noise: it does not know the batch exists. The tree's varies by 1.87 over the same range, and that variation is the whole effect.

## Where this already lives

Nothing above is new to factoring; it is the thing the sieves have always done, made explicit. The quadratic sieve does not test its candidates one at a time -- it walks each factor base prime through the interval, so the whole interval is tested for that prime in one pass. Round 33 measured the other side of that trade: CFRAC generates residues that are *smaller*, and so smooth more often, but has to divide each one on its own -- which is why its asymptotic constant is the worse of the two (`L[1/2, sqrt 2]` against the sieve's `L[1/2, 1]`), even though at the sizes measured there it was the faster of the two implementations.

The same argument scales up to whole factorisations. Coppersmith's batch number field sieve factors many numbers of the same size for less per number than one alone, by sharing the sieving and the linear algebra -- the same trick at the level of the algorithm rather than the arithmetic.

## What it does not do

Amortisation lowers the per-number cost of a batch. It does not lower the cost of *a* number, and factoring is asked about one number. The distinction is exactly the one round 37 drew: `k` correlated moduli fall to a lattice that cannot touch a single modulus, and a batch of numbers is cheaper each than one number alone. Both are real, both are measured here, and neither is an attack on the instance that matters.

That is the last exponent in this survey that moves. It moves in the batch size, not in `N`.
